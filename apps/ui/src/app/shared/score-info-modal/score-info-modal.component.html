ResuRank scores a job description against your resume using two independent methods — semantic embedding and keyword TF-IDF — then combines them into a single percentage.

Step 1 — Text preparation
  • Stopwords are removed. Common words and any words in your personal exclusion list are stripped out.
  • The job title gets extra weight. It is repeated twice before the description so title terms count more.
  • Text is sanitised for the embedding model. HTML, URLs, emoji, and Markdown are stripped before sending to the AI model.
  • Inputs are capped at 32,000 characters for the UI counter, and 6,000 characters (after sanitisation) before the embedding model.
Step 2 — Embedding score (semantic similarity)

Answers: do these two texts mean the same thing, even if they use different words?

Both texts are passed through a local AI model ({{ modelLabel() }}, ~{{ modelSizeMb }} MB, runs fully offline). The model converts each text into a vector representing its meaning. The score is the cosine similarity between the two vectors.

Good at catching paraphrases ("led a team" ↔ "people management"), but can find abstract similarity even when texts share no keywords — which is why it is not used alone.

Step 3 — TF-IDF score (keyword similarity)

Answers: do these two texts share the same specific words?

Builds a two-document keyword index and computes cosine similarity in keyword-weight space. Rare terms are weighted more than common ones ("Kubernetes" matters more than "experience").

  • Overlap bonus: up to +20 percentage points when your top 100 resume terms appear in the job description.
  • Term boosts: configured boost terms get their TF-IDF weight multiplied (only effective for terms already in your resume).
Step 4 — Combining the scores
score = 0.60 × embedding + 0.40 × TF-IDF

The embedding gets more weight because it captures meaning. The TF-IDF anchors the score to actual shared vocabulary.

Step 5 — Divergence adjustment

The embedding can find semantic similarity between any two professional texts even with zero keyword overlap. To correct for this, the embedding weight is smoothly reduced as TF-IDF approaches zero:

  • TF-IDF ≥ 15% — normal weights (60/40). No adjustment.
  • TF-IDF near 0% — embedding drops to 10%, TF-IDF rises to 90%. Collapses the score toward near-zero TF-IDF.
  • In between — smooth linear transition.

The "Divergence penalty" in the breakdown is the difference between the normal-weight score and the adjusted score.

Step 6 — Critical missing keywords (optional)

Off by default. The cosine and embedding steps already account for missing words indirectly, but they treat every keyword equally. This step lets you mark specific terms as critical so their absence from your resume actively reduces the score — useful when one missing keyword (e.g. "C#") is a real deal-breaker for the role.

  • Critical terms: add them in Settings, or mark any job-description keyword with the ⚑ button on its row in the keywords panel. The same list applies to every job description you score.
  • Importance tiers: each critical term is tagged Low, Medium (default), or High, which scales its weight in the penalty by 0.5×, 1×, or 2× respectively. Use this to say "C# matters more than Python" without changing the slider.
  • Formula: for the critical terms that appear in the current JD, penalty = (missing_weight ÷ total_weight) × max_reduction, where each term's weight is its JD-side TF-IDF multiplied by the importance tier. The "max reduction" slider in Settings caps how aggressive the penalty is (0–50%).
  • Critical terms that do not appear in the current JD are listed separately and do not affect the score.
Step 7 — Preference mismatch (optional)

Off by default. The other steps all ask does this resume fit the job? This one asks the inverse: does the job carry traits you want to avoid? In Settings you describe those traits in free text (e.g. "fast-paced startup with on-call rotations"). That text is embedded once and compared directly to the job description's embedding.

  • Why a separate input: adding "anti-preferences" to your resume would backfire — the embedding model can't represent negation, so the unwanted topic would actually pull the score up. Keeping the preference text on its own lets us subtract instead of add.
  • Baseline floor: any two pieces of English prose share a non-trivial cosine just for being professional text, so similarity below ~0.55 is ignored and the remaining range is rescaled to 0–1.
  • Formula: penalty = max(0, (similarity − 0.55) ÷ (1 − 0.55)) × max_reduction. The "max reduction" slider caps how aggressive the penalty is (0–50%).
  • Describe the trait itself, not its negation — the model matches on topic, not on the word "not".
Language detection

If more than 3% of alphabetic characters in the job description are non-ASCII, a warning is shown. The divergence adjustment also helps here since TF-IDF will typically be near zero for a foreign-language job.
